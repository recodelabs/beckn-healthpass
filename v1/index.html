HealthPass · beckn protocol · October 2026

HealthPass gives every patient a record. Beckn connects that record to every clinic, lab and pharmacy.

HealthPass is a health record that belongs to the patient and opens inside WhatsApp. Beckn is an open protocol that lets clinics, labs, pharmacies and patient apps find each other, agree on a service and send each other results, with no single company or ministry in the middle. This site explains why HealthPass is stronger on a beckn network than on its own, using two everyday examples: a lab test and a prescription.

One patient, four places

Take Wanjiru, a 34-year-old in Nairobi being treated for high blood pressure. Over a year she sees two clinics, has blood tests at a lab and collects medicine from a pharmacy. Her history ends up in four places that don’t talk to each other: one clinic’s computer system, the other clinic’s paper file, a lab result sent to her by SMS, and a photo of a prescription on her phone. Each new provider starts from nothing. Tests get repeated, and no one has the full list of what she takes.

Today

No shared record. Each provider keeps its own piece, and Wanjiru carries the rest on paper and on her phone.

With HealthPass

One record that Wanjiru owns, opened in WhatsApp. It fills in only where HealthPass has built a connection to that provider’s system.

HealthPass on a beckn network

Each provider connects once, to the network, and is listed in a shared registry. Results and prescriptions come back to her record from any of them.

The case in four points

  • HealthPass makes the record the patient’s. It holds her health summary in the international standard format, opens in an app she already uses, and logs everyone who looks at it.
  • On its own, it has to connect to providers one at a time. Each clinic, lab and pharmacy system needs its own link, and HealthPass has no way to check that a provider is licensed.
  • Beckn replaces those one-off links with a network. A provider joins once, is listed in a shared registry and signs every message, so any patient app can find it, order from it and trust its results.
  • The model is proven at national scale. India runs payments (UPI) and online commerce (ONDC, which is built on beckn) this way: a public body runs the shared rails and many companies compete to build the apps people use. Our opportunity is to write the health rules for such a network and run HealthPass on it.

01

HealthPass today

HealthPass gives each patient a personal health record that they control. It has three parts.

  • The record. A private, secure store for one patient’s health data. It uses FHIR, the international standard that modern clinic, lab and pharmacy systems already use to exchange health data. At its core is the International Patient Summary (IPS), a standard short summary of the patient: allergies, conditions, medicines, vaccinations and recent results.
  • WhatsApp as the way in. The patient opens the record from a link in WhatsApp, as a secure page inside the chat. There is no app to download, and it works on the phones people already have.
  • An access log. Every time someone opens the record, HealthPass logs who it was and when.

How a nurse sees the record today

  1. Wanjiru shows a QR code.She opens HealthPass in WhatsApp and shows the nurse the QR code on her screen.
  2. She gives the nurse a one-time PIN.She receives a short PIN in WhatsApp that works once. It shows she agreed to share her record for this visit.
  3. The nurse enters their own access code.HealthPass sends each health worker an access code over WhatsApp. The code expires after a few hours, so an old one can’t be reused.
  4. The nurse reads the summary and adds the visit.The access is written to the log.

Where it gets stuck on its own

  • It can’t tell who is a real health worker. The access code proves that someone got a code from HealthPass. It doesn’t prove they are a licensed nurse at a real clinic, because HealthPass has no shared list of licensed providers to check against. Sending and typing codes also adds a step to every visit.
  • Every clinic, lab and pharmacy has to be connected one by one. For results to arrive by themselves, HealthPass has to build a link to each provider’s software. A country has hundreds of these systems, so this is slow and costly, and it starts again in every new country.
  • So most care never reaches the record. When Wanjiru has a blood test at a lab HealthPass isn’t connected to, the result reaches her on paper or by SMS. Her record doesn’t change unless someone types it in.

What beckn changes

  • A shared register of who is licensed. Clinics, labs, pharmacies and health workers are listed in a registry with a digital key, and every message they send is signed with that key. HealthPass checks the signature instead of handing out codes.
  • Connect once. A lab connects its system to the network once. From then on, any patient app on the network can order tests from it, HealthPass included.
  • Records fill in as care happens. When a lab or pharmacy completes an order placed over the network, the result goes back to the patient’s record as part of the same exchange.

FHIR is the format of the record. Beckn is the set of rules for how independent organisations find each other, agree on a service and trust each other’s messages. The next section shows where this model has already worked at national scale.

03

Beckn in one diagram

Beckn is an open protocol that lets independent apps and providers find each other, agree on a service, and track it to completion. Any app on the network can transact with any provider without a bilateral integration and without a platform in the middle.

Beckn carries the transaction. Every message is signed by its sender, and the receiver answers with a counter-signed receipt, so both sides can prove who asked for what and when. FHIR and SMART on FHIR carry the clinical data. Patient data never travels inside a beckn message.

Each call is answered asynchronously by a matching callback, such as on_select or on_confirm. In beckn v2 (May 2026), providers host their own signed catalogues and discovery services crawl them, so no central gateway sits in the path18.

Wallet and app: HealthPass plays two roles

HealthPass is two things that are easy to blur together. On the network they are separate roles, and they can be switched separately.

The wallet: where the record is keptThe patient's FHIR record and International Patient Summary. Providers read from it and write back to it with SMART tokens, scoped by a FHIR Consent the patient approved. In the diagrams this is the "vault".
The app: what the patient talks toThe WhatsApp interface and agent that searches, books, orders and shows the record. In beckn terms it is the consumer node.

India's payments system works the same way. Your bank holds your money; that's the wallet. PhonePe or Google Pay is the app you pay with, and it holds no money. Because the two are separate, you can change apps and still pay the same shops the next day.

A patient on the network can switch in two ways:

  • Keep the wallet, change the app. Another app reads and writes the same HealthPass record through SMART on FHIR, with the patient's consent. The data doesn't move, much like using a different email program on the same mailbox.
  • Move the wallet. The patient exports their full FHIR record and IPS to another wallet. Kenya's Digital Health Act already calls for "shareable and portable personal health records"11.

Either way the patient keeps their identity, which comes from the national client registry rather than an app account. They also keep their providers, who join the network rather than any one app. HealthPass offers both roles, built so they can be separated. That is what makes "no lock-in" believable to governments and funders, and it lets other apps build on HealthPass wallets later.

    05

    How India did it: ABDM

    India's Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM), launched in September 2021, is the largest working example of shared health records built as public infrastructure. It has no central record store. Records stay with the hospital, clinic or lab that created them, and move only when the patient approves14.

    ABHA numberA 14-digit health ID. People create it by verifying with an Aadhaar or mobile one-time code, and it's voluntary. It holds no health data; records from different providers are linked to it24.978 million created, Sept 20267
    ABHA addressA readable handle such as priya@abdm. Patients use it to sign in to health-record apps and to approve requests for their records24.
    RegistriesA Health Facility Registry and a Healthcare Professionals Registry, so every party on the network can check who it is dealing with.533,000 facilities · 985,000 professionals, July 202622
    Consent managerPasses requests between providers and records what the patient approved. It never sees the clinical data21,28.

    Consent and routingFHIR dataPatient appInternal step

      Where the data lives

      Every hospital, clinic and lab keeps its own records in its own software. To join, that software must be certified for ABDM, so it can link visits to an ABHA and answer requests for records. Nothing is copied to a national database. The network only knows where records are, not what they say.

      PartyWhat it holdsWhat it never holds
      Hospital, clinic or labThe clinical records, in its own system
      Consent managerAn index of which providers hold records for each ABHA address, and every consent the patient has givenClinical data
      ABDM gatewayNothing lasting. It routes messages and checks that senders are registered.Clinical data
      Requesting providerA copy of what the patient approved, until the consent expiresAnything outside the consent
      Patient's appTheir ABHA address, their consents, and optionally their own copy of their records

      How one exchange works

      1. Records get linked to the ABHA.This happens in one of two ways. The hospital links a visit when it happens, often after the patient scans a QR code at the registration desk23. Or the patient finds older records from their app: they pick the hospital, the hospital matches them by name, age and phone number, and they confirm with a one-time code29. Either way, the consent manager learns that this hospital has records for this ABHA address. It doesn't learn what they say.
      2. A provider asks.The requesting clinic sends a consent request to the consent manager with the patient's ABHA address. It states the purpose, the record types (prescriptions, lab reports, discharge summaries and so on), the date range, and how long it wants to keep the data.
      3. The patient approves.In their app, the patient sees the request and can narrow it to particular hospitals, visits or record types. The consent manager then issues a signed consent artefact for each hospital involved. It lists exactly what may be shared and when the permission ends26.
      4. The requester asks the hospital for the data.The request quotes the consent artefact. It also carries a fresh one-time encryption key and an address where the hospital should send the data26.
      5. The hospital checks and sends.It verifies the consent's signature, scope and expiry, and pulls the approved records from its own system. It converts them into FHIR bundles using India's national profiles25, encrypts them with the requester's one-time key, and pushes them straight to the requester. It then tells the consent manager the transfer happened, for the audit trail. The gateway and the consent manager never see the content.
      6. The requester uses the data, then deletes it.It decrypts the records and shows them to the clinician. Each consent carries an expiry date, after which the requester must delete or anonymise the data27.
      What this design gets right
      • No central database to breach or to own
      • Providers keep control of their own records
      • The patient approves every share, and every share is logged
      What it costs
      • The hospital's system has to be online and certified when a request arrives
      • Every request waits on the patient, and most patients don't act
      • No one holds a complete history unless someone keeps a copy

      What HealthPass takes from this

      • Use the public rails; don't rebuild them. IDs and registries are public goods. In Kenya, HealthPass should use the client, facility and health-worker registries the Digital Health Agency already runs.
      • The approval step needs an app people already open. ABDM's weakest milestone is the one where the patient has to act. HealthPass lives in WhatsApp.
      • Keep the patient's own copy. In ABDM terms, HealthPass is a health-record app that stores the patient's copy as records are created. That gives the patient the complete history the federated model leaves out, and an IPS they can show at any clinic, including one that isn't connected to the exchange.

      Why a network

      The prescription shows the difference most clearly.

      Today

      • A photo can be edited, and nothing shows who wrote it.
      • The same prescription can be filled twice.
      • The prescriber never learns what was actually given.

      On the network

      • The signature and the prescriber's licence are checked against the registry.
      • Dispensing marks the prescription completed, so it can't be filled twice.
      • The dispense record, including any generic substitute, goes back to the prescriber.

      Being able to switch doesn't mean people will. On UPI, two apps handle about 79% of payments10. Those apps win by being better, though, and they can't hold people's money or the shops hostage, so leaving stays cheap. The same holds for health only if network rules require every wallet to export the record in standard form. That rule is part of the government's job.

      07

      The opportunity

      Kenya already has the public rails

      The Digital Health Act 2023 calls for "shareable and portable personal health records" (s.6(d))11. The Digital Health Agency runs client, health-worker and facility registries and a FHIR-based Shared Health Record12. Kenya's FHIR guides already cover e-prescriptions, with each dispense recorded against its prescription, as well as lab orders and patient summaries13,19.

      What's missing is the services layer on top: a common way for patient apps, clinics, labs and pharmacies to transact. A beckn network adds that layer, and HealthPass is a patient app on it. HealthPass works alongside the national health information exchange rather than replacing it.

      Who does what

      Government
      • Registries for patients, facilities and health workers
      • Network rules and certification
      • The health information exchange and data governance
      Private sector
      • Patient apps
      • Clinics, labs and pharmacies
      • Discovery services
      HealthPass team
      • The beckn health domain pack, aligned with FHIR and Kenya's guides
      • FHIR-to-beckn adapters for providers
      • HealthPass as the reference patient app
      • A pilot

      Why it is a business

      • Distribution. Every provider on the network is reachable without a sales and integration project for each one.
      • Revenue. Completed transactions (bookings, lab orders, filled prescriptions) can earn fees. A record on its own is hard to charge for.
      • Position. The team that writes the health domain pack and runs the reference app shapes how the standard works.
      • Precedent. Moving Tech built Namma Yatri, a commercial app on an open beckn network, to 150 million trips6. On UPI, two apps handle about 79% of all payments10. Open networks still produce large winners.
      • Blended funding. DPI funders and governments can pay for the public parts (the domain pack, open adapters, registry integration) while HealthPass earns commercial revenue.

      What gets built first

      1. A draft health domain pack covering lab orders, prescriptions and appointments.
      2. FHIR-to-beckn adapters, so providers on existing FHIR systems can join without rebuilding.
      3. A Kenya pilot with one clinic network, one lab and a few pharmacies.
      08

      Risks

      • Health networks have been slow. India's health network on beckn, UHI, was first proposed in 2021 and formally launched in June 2026, with five services live14,20. It routes through a central government gateway and has published no usage figures. Beckn v2 removes the need for a central gateway.
      • Subsidies don't create demand. ONDC retail orders fell from 6.5 million to 4.6 million a month after incentives were cut, while mobility, where a zero-commission app served real demand, grew to 56% of transactions15.
      • Patient approval is the hard step. In India's ABDM, consent-based sharing of records between facilities remains very low because the patient-approval loop is hard to close16. An app patients already use, on WhatsApp, is the part that's missing. That's where HealthPass fits.
      • Competitors can join the same network. The advantage has to come from WhatsApp reach, the patient agent, and being first.
      • The domain pack doesn't exist yet. We found no published mapping between beckn and FHIR for health. Writing one is real work.
      • Regulation. Kenya's Digital Health Agency is custodian of health data under s.22 of the Act, so HealthPass operates under its rules. The Act itself is under appeal; the Court of Appeal stayed the High Court ruling against it in September 202417.
